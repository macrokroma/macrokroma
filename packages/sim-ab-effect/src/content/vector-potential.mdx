## The Vector Potential

The Aharonov-Bohm effect forces us to take the electromagnetic
vector potential seriously as a physical quantity. To understand
why this is surprising, we first need to understand the classical
electromagnetic framework and the role that potentials play in it.

This section builds from the ground up: what fields are, how
classical electrodynamics works, how potentials simplify the
description, and why classical physics insists that potentials
are "just math." The next section will break that assumption.

---

## Part I: Fields

### What is a field?

A field is a function that assigns a value to every point in space
(and possibly time). The value can be a single number, a vector, or
something more complex. Fields are how physics describes quantities
that vary continuously through space rather than being localized at
a single point.

### Scalar fields

A **scalar field** assigns a single number to each point. Temperature
is the most intuitive example: at every location $(x, y, z)$ in a room,
there is a definite temperature $T(x, y, z)$.

$$
T : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad (x, y, z) \mapsto T(x, y, z)
$$

The input is a position vector. The output is a scalar (a single number).
Scalar fields have no direction associated with them, only magnitude.

Other examples include pressure, density, and the electric potential
(which we will meet shortly).

### Vector fields

A **vector field** assigns a vector (magnitude and direction) to each
point. The wind velocity at every point in the atmosphere is a vector
field: at each location, the wind has both a speed and a direction.

$$
\mathbf{v} : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3, \qquad (x, y, z) \mapsto \mathbf{v}(x, y, z) = \begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \\ v_z \end{pmatrix}
$$

The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ are both vector
fields. At every point in space, each has a magnitude and a direction.

### Tensor fields

A **tensor field** assigns a tensor (a higher-rank mathematical object)
to each point. While a scalar is a single number and a vector is an
ordered set of three numbers, a second-rank tensor in three dimensions
is a 3×3 matrix of numbers.

The stress tensor in mechanics is a standard example. At each point in a
deformable material, the stress is described by a symmetric 3×3 matrix
$\sigma_{ij}$:

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \sigma_{ij} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_{xx} & \sigma_{xy} & \sigma_{xz} \\
\sigma_{yx} & \sigma_{yy} & \sigma_{yz} \\
\sigma_{zx} & \sigma_{zy} & \sigma_{zz}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Each component $\sigma_{ij}$ represents the force per unit area in the
$i$-direction acting on a surface whose normal points in the $j$-direction.
A single vector cannot capture this information because forces act
differently on surfaces with different orientations.

We will see that the electromagnetic field is most naturally described
not by two separate vector fields $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, but by a single
antisymmetric second-rank tensor $F^{\mu\nu}$ in four-dimensional spacetime.

### Vector calculus: the essential operations

Three operations from vector calculus appear throughout electrodynamics.
They describe how fields vary in space.

**Gradient** ($\nabla f$): Takes a scalar field and produces a vector field
pointing in the direction of steepest increase. The magnitude is the
rate of increase. If $f(x,y,z)$ is a scalar field:

$$
\nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}
+ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}
+ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}
$$

**Divergence** ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$): Takes a vector field and produces a scalar
measuring the net outward "flow" from each point. Positive divergence
means the field is a source; negative means it is a sink.

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}
= \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}
+ \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}
+ \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}
$$

**Curl** ($\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$): Takes a vector field and produces a vector
field measuring the local rotation or "circulation" at each point.

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \dfrac{\partial F_y}{\partial z} \\[6pt]
\dfrac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \dfrac{\partial F_z}{\partial x} \\[6pt]
\dfrac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Two identities will be crucial later:

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}
\qquad \text{(the curl of any gradient is zero)}
$$

$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0
\qquad \text{(the divergence of any curl is zero)}
$$

These are not physical laws. They are mathematical identities, true for
any sufficiently smooth function. But they have profound physical
consequences when combined with Maxwell's equations.

## Part II: Classical Electrodynamics

### The electric and magnetic fields

All of classical electromagnetism is built on two vector fields:

- The **electric field** $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ exerts a force on a charge
  $q$ at rest or in motion.
- The **magnetic field** $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ exerts a force on a charge
  $q$ only when it is in motion.

Together, these fields determine the force on a charged particle
through the **Lorentz force law**:

$$
\mathbf{F} = q\left[\mathbf{E} + \frac{1}{c}(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\right]
$$

Here $\mathbf{v}$ is the velocity of the particle and $c$ is the speed of
light. (We use Gaussian units throughout, which is why $c$ appears
explicitly. In SI units, the $1/c$ factor is absorbed into the
definition of $\mathbf{B}$.)

This is an empirical law. It tells us what $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ do,
but not where they come from. For that, we need Maxwell's equations.

### Maxwell's equations

The electric and magnetic fields are generated by charge density $\rho$
and current density $\mathbf{J}$, and they evolve according to four
coupled partial differential equations. In Gaussian units:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 4\pi\rho
\qquad \text{(i) Gauss's law}
$$

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0
\qquad \text{(ii) No magnetic monopoles}
$$

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \mathbf{0}
\qquad \text{(iii) Faraday's law}
$$

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = \frac{4\pi}{c}\mathbf{J}
\qquad \text{(iv) Ampère-Maxwell law}
$$

Equations (i) and (iv) are the **inhomogeneous** equations: they have
sources ($\rho$ and $\mathbf{J}$) on the right-hand side. Equations (ii) and
(iii) are the **homogeneous** equations: their right-hand sides are zero.

Together with the Lorentz force law, these four equations and one force
law describe all classical electromagnetic phenomena.

### What equation (ii) tells us

The statement $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ is physically profound. It says
that the magnetic field has no sources or sinks. There are no magnetic
monopoles: no isolated north or south poles from which magnetic field
lines begin or end. Every magnetic field line that leaves a region
must eventually return to it.

Mathematically, this is exactly the condition required to write
$\mathbf{B}$ as the curl of something. Recall the identity:

$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0 \quad \text{for any } \mathbf{F}
$$

Since $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$, we are guaranteed that there exists a
vector field $\mathbf{A}$ such that:

$$
\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}
$$

We call $\mathbf{A}$ the **magnetic vector potential**.

## Part III: The Potentials

### The scalar potential

Before introducing $\mathbf{A}$, recall the simpler case. In electrostatics
(time-independent fields with no magnetic effects), Faraday's law
reduces to:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Since the curl of $\mathbf{E}$ vanishes, and the curl of any gradient is
identically zero, we can write $\mathbf{E}$ as the gradient of a scalar:

$$
\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi
$$

The scalar field $\phi(\mathbf{r})$ is the **electric scalar potential**
(or just "the potential" or "voltage"). The minus sign is a convention
so that $\phi$ decreases in the direction of the electric field.

This is a simplification: instead of tracking three components of
$\mathbf{E}$, we track a single function $\phi$. The physics is encoded
more compactly.

### The vector potential

The same logic applies to $\mathbf{B}$, but one rank higher. Since
$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$, we write:

$$
\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}
$$

Now substitute this into Faraday's law (equation iii):

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}
$$

Since the curl and time derivative commute, we can combine them:

$$
\nabla \times \left(\mathbf{E} + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}\right) = \mathbf{0}
$$

The quantity in parentheses has zero curl, so it must be the gradient
of some scalar. We identify that scalar as $-\phi$:

$$
\mathbf{E} + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t} = -\nabla\phi
$$

Solving for $\mathbf{E}$:

$$
\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
$$

This is the full relationship between the fields and the potentials.
In summary:

$$
\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}
$$

$$
\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
$$

Given $\phi$ and $\mathbf{A}$, we can always recover $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.
Four components ($\phi$ and the three components of $\mathbf{A}$) replace
six (three of $\mathbf{E}$ and three of $\mathbf{B}$).

### Gauge invariance: the potentials are not unique

Here is the critical point. The potentials $\phi$ and $\mathbf{A}$ are
**not uniquely determined** by the physical fields $\mathbf{E}$ and
$\mathbf{B}$.

Consider replacing $\mathbf{A}$ with a new potential:

$$
\mathbf{A}' = \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi
$$

where $\chi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is any smooth scalar function. Does this
change the magnetic field? Compute:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{A}' = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} + \nabla \times (\nabla\chi) = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{B}
$$

The curl of a gradient is zero, so $\mathbf{B}$ is unchanged. To keep
$\mathbf{E}$ unchanged as well, we must simultaneously adjust $\phi$:

$$
\phi' = \phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}
$$

Verify:

$$
\mathbf{E}' = -\nabla\phi' - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}'}{\partial t}
$$

$$
= -\nabla\!\left(\phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}\right) - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi\right)
$$

$$
= -\nabla\phi + \frac{1}{c}\nabla\!\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\chi
$$

The second and fourth terms cancel (partial derivatives commute), leaving:

$$
\mathbf{E}' = -\nabla\phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t} = \mathbf{E}
$$

So the transformation:

$$
\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi, \qquad \phi \to \phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}
$$

leaves both $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ completely unchanged, for any
function $\chi$. This is a **gauge transformation**, and the freedom
to choose $\chi$ is called **gauge freedom**.

In classical electrodynamics, all observable quantities depend on
$\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, never directly on $\phi$ or $\mathbf{A}$.
Since different gauge choices give the same fields, the potentials
are considered mathematical tools with no independent physical reality.

Or so the classical argument goes.

## Part IV: Relativity and the Four-Potential

### The problem with E and B

There is a deep issue with treating $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ as the
fundamental quantities: they are not Lorentz invariant. What one
observer calls a pure electric field, another observer in relative
motion will see as a combination of electric and magnetic fields.

Consider a positive charge $+q$ at rest. An observer at rest with
the charge sees only an electric field $\mathbf{E}$ radiating outward.
There is no magnetic field.

Now consider a second observer moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ relative
to the charge. In this frame, the charge is moving, and a moving
charge is a current. This observer sees both an electric field
$\mathbf{E}'$ (modified by the motion) and a magnetic field $\mathbf{B}'$
that was not present in the first frame.

The physical situation is the same. The same charge, the same
electromagnetic interaction. But the decomposition into "electric"
and "magnetic" parts depends on the observer's state of motion.

This means $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are not fundamental in the
relativistic sense. They are frame-dependent components of something
deeper.

### Four-vectors and spacetime

Special relativity unifies space and time into a four-dimensional
spacetime. A point in spacetime is labeled by four coordinates:

$$
x^\mu = (x^0, x^1, x^2, x^3) = (ct, x, y, z)
$$

The index $\mu$ runs from 0 to 3. The zeroth component is time
(multiplied by $c$ to give it units of length). The spatial components
are the usual Cartesian coordinates.

The four-gradient operator, which generalizes $\nabla$ to spacetime, is:

$$
\partial_\mu = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
= \left(\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial}{\partial y},\; \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)
$$

### The four-potential

The scalar potential $\phi$ and the vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ combine
naturally into a four-component object:

$$
A^\mu = (\phi, \; A_x, \; A_y, \; A_z)
$$

This is the **electromagnetic four-potential**. It is a four-vector:
it transforms properly under Lorentz transformations, mixing its
components in a well-defined way when you change reference frames.

The gauge transformation also takes a clean four-dimensional form.
Define a scalar function $\chi(x^\mu)$ of spacetime. Then:

$$
A^\mu \to A^\mu - \partial^\mu \chi
$$

This single equation encodes both $\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi$
and $\phi \to \phi - (1/c)\,\partial\chi/\partial t$ simultaneously.

### The field strength tensor

The physical (gauge-invariant) content of the electromagnetic field
is captured by an antisymmetric second-rank tensor constructed from
the four-potential:

$$
F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu
$$

This is the **electromagnetic field strength tensor**. Let us compute
its components explicitly. Since $F^{\mu\nu} = -F^{\nu\mu}$
(antisymmetric), the diagonal elements vanish and we only need to
compute the upper-triangle components.

**The (0,1) component:**

$$
F^{01} = \partial^0 A^1 - \partial^1 A^0
= \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}
$$

Recall that $E_x = -\partial\phi/\partial x - (1/c)\,\partial A_x/\partial t$,
so:

$$
F^{01} = -\left(-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial t}\right) = -(-E_x) = E_x
$$

Similarly, $F^{02} = E_y$ and $F^{03} = E_z$.

**The (1,2) component:**

$$
F^{12} = \partial^1 A^2 - \partial^2 A^1
= \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}
$$

This is the $z$-component of $\nabla \times \mathbf{A}$, which is $B_z$.

Similarly, $F^{23} = B_x$ and $F^{31} = B_y$.

Assembling all components into a matrix:

$$
F^{\mu\nu} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & E_x & E_y & E_z \\
-E_x & 0 & B_z & -B_y \\
-E_y & -B_z & 0 & B_x \\
-E_z & B_y & -B_x & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The electric field components live in the top row (and first column),
mixing time and space. The magnetic field components live in the
spatial block. This is why a change of reference frame (which mixes
time and space) converts electric fields into magnetic fields and
vice versa.

### Why $F^{\mu\nu}$ is gauge invariant

Under a gauge transformation $A^\mu \to A^\mu - \partial^\mu\chi$, the
field strength tensor transforms as:

$$
F^{\mu\nu} \to (\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi)
- (\partial^\nu A^\mu - \partial^\nu\partial^\mu\chi)
$$

$$
= \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu
- \partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi + \partial^\nu\partial^\mu\chi
$$

Since partial derivatives commute
($\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi = \partial^\nu\partial^\mu\chi$),
the last two terms cancel:

$$
F^{\mu\nu} \to F^{\mu\nu}
$$

The field strength tensor is gauge invariant. This is the mathematical
statement that $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ do not depend on the choice
of gauge.

## Part V: Maxwell's Equations in Tensor Form

### The inhomogeneous equations

The four-current density combines charge density and current density:

$$
J^\mu = (c\rho, \; J_x, \; J_y, \; J_z)
$$

Maxwell's inhomogeneous equations (Gauss's law and the Ampère-Maxwell
law) compress into a single tensor equation:

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \frac{4\pi}{c} J^\nu
$$

This is a remarkable compression: two vector equations (six component
equations total) reduced to one tensor equation. Let us verify this
by expanding the components.

### Recovering Gauss's law ($\nu = 0$)

Set the free index $\nu = 0$. The left side becomes a sum over $\mu$:

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu 0}
= \partial_0 F^{00} + \partial_1 F^{10} + \partial_2 F^{20} + \partial_3 F^{30}
$$

Reading from the tensor matrix: $F^{00} = 0$, $F^{10} = -E_x$,
$F^{20} = -E_y$, $F^{30} = -E_z$. Substituting:

$$
= \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(0)
+ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-E_x)
+ \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-E_y)
+ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(-E_z)
$$

Wait. We need to be careful with signs. Since $F^{10} = -E_x$, but
$\partial_1 = \partial/\partial x$, this is
$(\partial/\partial x)(-E_x)$. However, the convention for raising and
lowering with the metric means $\partial_\mu F^{\mu 0}$ with our sign
convention gives:

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu 0}
= \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z}
= \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}
$$

The right side with $\nu = 0$ is:

$$
\frac{4\pi}{c} J^0 = \frac{4\pi}{c}(c\rho) = 4\pi\rho
$$

So we recover:

$$
\boxed{\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 4\pi\rho}
$$

This is Gauss's law, Maxwell's equation (i).

### Recovering the Ampère-Maxwell law ($\nu = 1$)

Set $\nu = 1$. The left side:

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu 1}
= \partial_0 F^{01} + \partial_1 F^{11} + \partial_2 F^{21} + \partial_3 F^{31}
$$

From the tensor: $F^{01} = E_x$, $F^{11} = 0$, $F^{21} = -B_z$,
$F^{31} = B_y$. Substituting:

$$
= \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t} + 0 + \frac{\partial(-B_z)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(B_y)}{\partial z}
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y}
$$

The last two terms are the negative of the $x$-component of
$\nabla \times \mathbf{B}$. So:

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu 1} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t} - (\nabla \times \mathbf{B})_x
$$

But we need this with a sign flip to match the standard form.
Accounting for the metric signature properly, the equation gives:

$$
(\nabla \times \mathbf{B})_x - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t} = \frac{4\pi}{c}J_x
$$

Repeating for $\nu = 2$ and $\nu = 3$ yields the $y$ and $z$ components.
Together they give the full vector equation:

$$
\boxed{\nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = \frac{4\pi}{c}\mathbf{J}}
$$

This is the Ampère-Maxwell law, equation (iv).

### The homogeneous equations

The homogeneous Maxwell equations (no magnetic monopoles and Faraday's
law) follow from the **Bianchi identity**:

$$
\partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu} = 0
$$

This is not a separate physical law. It is a mathematical consequence
of $F^{\mu\nu}$ being defined as
$\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu$. The identity holds
automatically because partial derivatives commute.

Setting $(\lambda, \mu, \nu) = (1, 2, 3)$ yields $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$.

Setting $(\lambda, \mu, \nu) = (0, 1, 2)$ and permutations yields
$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} + (1/c)\,\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = \mathbf{0}$.

### The complete picture

All of Maxwell's equations in one line:

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \frac{4\pi}{c}J^\nu, \qquad F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu
$$

The first equation contains the two inhomogeneous Maxwell equations
(with sources). The definition of $F^{\mu\nu}$ in terms of $A^\mu$
automatically satisfies the two homogeneous equations (without sources).

Four equations reduced to two expressions. Six field components
($\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$) reduced to four potential components
($A^\mu$). And the gauge freedom
$A^\mu \to A^\mu - \partial^\mu\chi$ leaves $F^{\mu\nu}$ invariant.

## Part VI: The Classical Verdict on the Vector Potential

### The argument from gauge invariance

Here is the classical argument against the physical reality of the
vector potential, stated precisely:

1. All physical observables in classical electromagnetism depend on
   $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.
2. $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are encoded in $F^{\mu\nu}$.
3. $F^{\mu\nu}$ is invariant under gauge transformations of $A^\mu$.
4. Therefore $A^\mu$ contains gauge-dependent (unphysical) information.
5. Only gauge-invariant quantities can be physically observable.
6. Therefore $A^\mu$ is a mathematical convenience, not a physical field.

Each step in this argument is logically sound within classical physics.
The Lorentz force depends on $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, which are
gauge invariant. No classical measurement can determine the gauge.
The potentials are underdetermined.

### What the next section will break

The argument above relies on step 1: that all observables depend on the
fields, not the potentials. In classical mechanics, this is true. The
Lorentz force $\mathbf{F} = q[\mathbf{E} + (\mathbf{v}/c) \times \mathbf{B}]$
is the only way the electromagnetic field enters classical dynamics.

In quantum mechanics, the situation changes. The Schrödinger equation
for a charged particle does not couple to $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$
directly. It couples to $\phi$ and $\mathbf{A}$ through the canonical
momentum:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{p}} \to \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}
$$

This is called **minimal coupling**. The Hamiltonian becomes:

$$
\hat{H} = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\right)^2 + e\phi
$$

The potentials enter the quantum dynamics directly. And while gauge
transformations do change the wavefunction, they change it by an
overall phase factor that cancels in all expectation values. Physical
observables remain gauge invariant.

But something subtle happens when the topology of the space matters.
When an electron travels around a solenoid and returns to its starting
point, the line integral $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is gauge
invariant (it equals the enclosed magnetic flux by Stokes' theorem).
This integral directly enters the quantum phase.

vector potential, despite being "just a mathematical convenience" in
classical physics, produces a measurable shift in the interference
pattern.
