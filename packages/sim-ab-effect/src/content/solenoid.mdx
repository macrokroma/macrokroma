## The Solenoid and the AB Phase

We now have all the ingredients. From Section 1, we know how
double-slit interference works: two paths, a phase difference,
and an intensity pattern that depends on that difference. From
Section 2, we know that the vector potential A is classically
considered a mathematical convenience, not a physical field.

This section brings them together. We place a solenoid between
the slits and show that the vector potential, despite producing
no force on the electrons, shifts the interference pattern.

## The Setup

### An ideal solenoid

Consider an infinitely long solenoid of radius $R$ carrying a
steady current. Inside the solenoid, the magnetic field is
uniform and parallel to the axis:

$$
\mathbf{B} = B_0 \,\hat{\mathbf{z}} \qquad (r < R)
$$

Outside the solenoid, the magnetic field vanishes:

$$
\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0} \qquad (r > R)
$$

This is a standard result from magnetostatics, derived by
applying Ampère's law to rectangular loops that cross the
solenoid boundary.

The total magnetic flux through the solenoid is:

$$
\Phi = B_0 \cdot \pi R^2
$$

### The vector potential outside

Even though $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$ outside the solenoid, the
vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ is not zero there. To find A outside,
we use Stokes' theorem. For any closed loop $\mathcal{C}$
encircling the solenoid:

$$
\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}
= \iint_{S} (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}
= \iint_{S} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S}
= \Phi
$$

The surface integral of B picks up the full flux $\Phi$ because
the surface bounded by $\mathcal{C}$ passes through the interior
of the solenoid where $\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

For a circular loop of radius $r > R$ centered on the solenoid
axis, symmetry demands that A is purely azimuthal and constant
in magnitude around the loop:

$$
\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}
= A_\theta \cdot 2\pi r = \Phi
$$

Solving for $A_\theta$:

$$
A_\theta(r) = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi r} \qquad (r > R)
$$

In vector form, using the azimuthal unit vector
$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = (-\sin\theta,\, \cos\theta,\, 0)$:

$$
\mathbf{A}(r, \theta) = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi r}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\qquad (r > R)
$$

Let us verify that this gives $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$ outside.
The curl in cylindrical coordinates for a purely azimuthal field
$\mathbf{A} = A_\theta(r)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r A_\theta)}{\partial r}\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}
$$

Substituting $A_\theta = \Phi/(2\pi r)$:

$$
r A_\theta = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi} = \text{constant}
$$

The derivative of a constant is zero:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{r} \cdot 0 = \mathbf{0}
\qquad (r > R) \quad \checkmark
$$

So the curl of A vanishes outside the solenoid, confirming
$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$ there. But A itself is nonzero and
circulates everywhere outside.

This is the geometry we explored in the solenoid simulation
in the previous section. The key point bears repeating: the
electrons travel in a region where $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$
but $\mathbf{A} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

### Inside the solenoid

For completeness, inside the solenoid ($r < R$), we need
$\nabla \times \mathbf{A} = B_0\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. By symmetry,
A is again azimuthal. Using Stokes' theorem on a circle of
radius $r < R$:

$$
A_\theta \cdot 2\pi r = B_0 \cdot \pi r^2
$$

$$
A_\theta(r) = \frac{B_0 \, r}{2} = \frac{\Phi \, r}{2\pi R^2}
\qquad (r < R)
$$

Inside, A grows linearly with $r$. Outside, it falls off as $1/r$.
At the boundary $r = R$, both expressions give
$A_\theta(R) = \Phi/(2\pi R)$, confirming continuity.

## The Quantum Phase

### Minimal coupling

In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian for a charged particle
(charge $e$, mass $m$) in an electromagnetic field is obtained
by the **minimal coupling** prescription:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{p}} \to \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}
$$

The Schrödinger equation becomes:

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t}
= \left[
\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\right)^2
+ e\phi
\right] \Psi
$$

For the AB setup, there is no electric field ($\phi = 0$) and
the vector potential is time-independent. The key question is:
what is the effect of A on the wavefunction?

### The phase factor

Consider a particle moving from point $\mathbf{x}_0$ to point
$\mathbf{x}$ along some path. If $\Psi_0$ is the wavefunction
in the absence of A (the free-particle solution), then the
wavefunction in the presence of A is:

$$
\Psi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \Psi_0(\mathbf{x}, t) \,
\exp\!\left(\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}}
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}') \cdot d\mathbf{x}'\right)
$$

Let us derive this. Start with the Schrödinger equation in the
Coulomb gauge ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0$, $\phi = 0$):

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t}
= \frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\right)^2 \Psi
$$

Write $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = (\hbar/i)\nabla$ and expand the square:

$$
\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\right)^2 \Psi
= -\hbar^2 \nabla^2 \Psi
+ \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\left(\nabla \cdot (\mathbf{A}\Psi) + \mathbf{A} \cdot \nabla\Psi\right)
+ \frac{e^2}{c^2}A^2 \Psi
$$

In the Coulomb gauge, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0$, so
$\nabla \cdot (\mathbf{A}\Psi) = \mathbf{A} \cdot \nabla\Psi$.
This simplifies to:

$$
\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\right)^2 \Psi
= -\hbar^2 \nabla^2 \Psi
+ \frac{2i\hbar e}{c}\mathbf{A} \cdot \nabla\Psi
+ \frac{e^2}{c^2}A^2 \Psi
$$

Now make the substitution $\Psi = \Psi_0 \, e^{i\chi}$ where
$\chi(\mathbf{x}) = (e/\hbar c)\int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}'$.
Computing $\nabla\Psi$:

$$
\nabla\Psi = (\nabla\Psi_0)\,e^{i\chi} + \Psi_0\,(i\nabla\chi)\,e^{i\chi}
$$

Since $\nabla\chi = (e/\hbar c)\mathbf{A}$, the A-dependent terms
cancel, and $\Psi_0$ satisfies the free Schrödinger equation:

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi_0}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 \Psi_0
$$

This confirms that the effect of a static vector potential on a
quantum particle is a **position-dependent phase factor**:

$$
\boxed{
\Psi(\mathbf{x}) = \Psi_0(\mathbf{x}) \,
\exp\!\left(\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}}
\mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}'\right)
}
$$

The amplitude $|\Psi|^2 = |\Psi_0|^2$ is unchanged. A single
particle traveling along a single path acquires a phase that has
no observable consequence (it is a global phase). The magic
happens when two paths are involved.

## Two Paths, Two Phases

### The AB experiment

An electron beam is split into two paths that pass on opposite
sides of the solenoid and recombine at a detection screen,
exactly as in the double-slit setup from Section 1.

Path $a$ goes above the solenoid. Path $b$ goes below. Each
path picks up a phase from the line integral of A:

$$
\Psi_a = \Psi_{a_0} \,
\exp\!\left(\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \int_a \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}\right)
$$

$$
\Psi_b = \Psi_{b_0} \,
\exp\!\left(\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \int_b \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}\right)
$$

### The total wavefunction

The total wavefunction at the screen is the superposition:

$$
\Psi = \Psi_a + \Psi_b
$$

Factor out the phase from path $b$:

$$
\Psi = \left[
\Psi_{a_0} \, e^{i\frac{e}{\hbar c}\left(\int_a \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x} - \int_b \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}\right)}
+ \Psi_{b_0}
\right]
e^{i\frac{e}{\hbar c}\int_b \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}}
$$

The overall phase factor $e^{i(e/\hbar c)\int_b \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}}$
is a global phase that drops out when we compute $|\Psi|^2$.
What remains is a **relative phase** between the two paths:

$$
\Delta\varphi = \frac{e}{\hbar c}\left(
\int_a \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}
- \int_b \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}
\right)
$$

### Stokes' theorem and the closed loop

The difference of the two line integrals is a line integral
around a closed loop (path $a$ forward, then path $b$ backward):

$$
\int_a \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}
- \int_b \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}
= \oint_{\mathcal{C}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}
$$

By Stokes' theorem:

$$
\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{x}
= \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}
= \iint_S \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S}
= \Phi
$$

The surface $S$ bounded by the closed loop $\mathcal{C}$ encloses
the solenoid, so the surface integral captures the full flux
$\Phi$, even though $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$ along the paths
themselves.

Therefore the AB phase is:

$$
\boxed{
\Delta\varphi = \frac{e}{\hbar c}\,\Phi = \frac{2\pi\Phi}{\Phi_0}
}
$$

where $\Phi_0 = hc/e = 2\pi\hbar c/e$ is the **magnetic flux quantum**.

### The probability density

The measured intensity at the screen is:

$$
P = |\Psi|^2 = |\Psi_{a_0}|^2 + |\Psi_{b_0}|^2
+ 2\,\text{Re}\!\left(\Psi_{a_0}^* \Psi_{b_0} \, e^{i\Delta\varphi}\right)
$$

Compare this with the zero-flux result from Section 1:

$$
P_0 = |\Psi_{a_0}|^2 + |\Psi_{b_0}|^2
+ 2\,\text{Re}\!\left(\Psi_{a_0}^* \Psi_{b_0}\right)
$$

The only difference is the factor $e^{i\Delta\varphi}$ in the
interference term. This shifts the fringe pattern laterally by
an amount proportional to the enclosed flux.

### Gauge invariance of the result

The AB phase $\Delta\varphi = (e/\hbar c)\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$
is a closed-loop integral. Under a gauge transformation
$\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi$:

$$
\oint (\mathbf{A} + \nabla\chi) \cdot d\mathbf{l}
= \oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint \nabla\chi \cdot d\mathbf{l}
$$

The second integral vanishes because the line integral of a
gradient around any closed loop is zero (for single-valued $\chi$):

$$
\oint \nabla\chi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \chi(\mathbf{x}_{\text{end}}) - \chi(\mathbf{x}_{\text{start}}) = 0
$$

So $\Delta\varphi$ is gauge invariant. The AB phase depends on
the enclosed flux, not on the particular gauge choice for A.
Different gauges give different A fields (as we saw in the
gauge transformation simulation), but they all agree on the
value of $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \Phi$.

## Flux Quantization

### The periodicity

The interference pattern depends on $\Delta\varphi$ through the
cosine in the interference term. Since $\cos(\alpha + 2\pi n) = \cos\alpha$
for any integer $n$, the pattern is periodic in the flux:

$$
\Phi \to \Phi + n\Phi_0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\text{same interference pattern}
$$

At $\Phi = 0$, the standard double-slit pattern appears. As
$\Phi$ increases from zero, the fringes shift. At $\Phi = \Phi_0$,
the pattern has shifted by exactly one fringe spacing and looks
identical to the $\Phi = 0$ case.

### The flux quantum

The fundamental period is set by:

$$
\Phi_0 = \frac{hc}{e} = \frac{2\pi\hbar c}{e}
\approx 4.14 \times 10^{-7} \;\text{G}\cdot\text{cm}^2
$$

This is a remarkably small quantity. The AB effect is observable
because electron wavelengths are short enough (and experimental
techniques precise enough) to resolve phase shifts of order
$2\pi$.

## Why This Matters

The AB effect demonstrates three things:

**The vector potential is physical.** The electrons never encounter
$\mathbf{B}$. They travel through a region where $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$
and the Lorentz force is zero. Yet their interference pattern
shifts. The shift depends on $\oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$,
which equals the enclosed flux. Classical electrodynamics says A is
a mathematical tool. Quantum mechanics says it has measurable
consequences.

**Topology matters.** The effect depends on the topology of the
space: the paths go around the solenoid, forming a loop that
encloses flux. If both paths were on the same side of the solenoid
(not enclosing it), the phase difference would be zero. It is the
act of encircling the flux tube that produces the effect.

**Locality is subtle.** The electrons interact with A at their
own position (A is nonzero where they travel). In that sense, the
interaction is local. But the value of A outside the solenoid is
determined by the flux inside, where the electrons never go. The
information about the enclosed flux is carried by A to the
electrons' location, even though B does not reach them.
