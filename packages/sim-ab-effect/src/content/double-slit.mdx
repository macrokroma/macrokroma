## The Double-Slit Experiment

Before we can understand the Aharonov–Bohm effect, we need to understand
what happens when electrons pass through two slits — no magnetic fields,
no vector potentials, just quantum mechanics at its most fundamental.

### Wavefunctions and superposition

An electron isn't a tiny ball that goes through one slit or the other.
It's described by a **wavefunction** $\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ — a complex-valued
field that fills all of space. The probability of detecting the electron
at position $\mathbf{r}$ is the squared magnitude of the wavefunction:

$$
P(\mathbf{r}) = |\psi(\mathbf{r})|^2
$$

When the electron encounters two slits, the wavefunction splits. After the
slits, the total wavefunction is a superposition of the contributions
from each slit:

$$
\psi_{\text{total}} = \psi_1 + \psi_2
$$

### From path difference to phase difference

Each slit acts as a point source of spherical waves. A wave from slit $j$
arriving at position $x$ on the screen has traveled a distance $r_j$ and
accumulated a phase $\phi_j = k \cdot r_j$, where $k = 2\pi / \lambda$
is the wave number:

$$
\psi_j(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_j}} \, e^{i k r_j}
$$

The key quantity is the **path difference** $\Delta r = r_2 - r_1$ between
the two routes. This creates a **phase difference**:

$$
\Delta\phi = k \cdot \Delta r = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(r_2 - r_1)
$$

### The intensity pattern

The probability density — what we actually measure on the screen — comes
from squaring the total amplitude:

$$
I(x) = |\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}(\psi_1^* \psi_2)
$$

That cross-term $2\,\text{Re}(\psi_1^* \psi_2)$ is the **interference term**.
It oscillates as a function of position, creating the characteristic pattern
of bright and dark fringes. When the phase difference is a multiple of $2\pi$,
the waves reinforce. When it's an odd multiple of $\pi$, they cancel.
